## Outcome

You can record a Frequency Analyzer reading from your own remote, next to a control press that gives no reading, together with your Flipper's firmware version, region and SD card status.

By the end you can:

- set up the device, record its firmware version and region code, and confirm the microSD card works;
- find your way into the Sub-GHz menu and back to the desktop in the simulator, and read a `.sub` file line by line;
- explain what a Frequency Analyzer reading does and does not prove, using a control press as evidence.

Radio feels abstract until you watch a reading appear when you press a doorbell. So that is the job today: press the doorbell and write down what happened.

## Before you start

- **Prerequisites:** none. If words like "frequency" and "hertz" are new, skim the [primer](/primer) first.
- **Time:** about 90 minutes, including the quiz and notebook entry. The firmware update (Flipper's documentation says about 10 minutes through the mobile app) is the slowest part, so start it first or do it the day before.
- **What transmits:** nothing on the Flipper. Your remote transmits when you press it, which is what it was built to do.
- **Scope:** your own device, your own space, your own remotes.

### Set up and keep a lab record

1. Charge the Flipper over USB-C. To switch it on, hold <kbd>Back</kbd> for 3 seconds.
2. Insert a microSD card. The Flipper accepts cards up to 256 GB; 16 to 32 GB from a known brand is plenty. Back up anything on the card before you format it at <Flip>Settings → Storage → Format SD Card</Flip>. Push the card in until it clicks; a card that works loose is a common reason a save fails. If you format the card later, run the firmware update again, because the firmware's assets live on the card.
3. Update the firmware with qFlipper (desktop, over USB) or the Flipper Mobile App (phone, over Bluetooth). Keep the card inserted: the update needs it. Choose the **Release** channel, which Flipper recommends for general use.
4. Record two facts. Holding <kbd>Down</kbd> on the desktop opens Device Info, which shows the firmware and hardware details and the ISO region code. Flipper's frequencies page names this screen as the place to read the region. <Flip>Settings → About</Flip> also shows hardware, region and firmware information, so use whichever screen shows the version number on yours.

### Choose a documented starting point

Use official firmware for this course. If you already run Momentum, a community firmware, record its version and keep going; the radio principles are the same.

<Callout variant="warn" title="Leave region settings alone">
Momentum's [README](https://github.com/Next-Flip/Momentum-Firmware) lists a setting under Momentum Settings → Protocols that extends Sub-GHz to 281–361, 378–481 and 749–962 MHz, wider than the chip's documented bands. Other firmware may offer further ways to widen the transmit range. Leave region and range settings at their defaults: this course never needs them, and widening the range can let you transmit where you are not allowed to.
</Callout>

<Callout variant="legal" title="One rule, from the first press">
Everything in Signal Path uses devices **you own**, in a space **you control**. Today you only receive, but receiving is not a blanket permission: a receiver can still collect other people's private data, and the rules on intercepting communications are separate from the rules on transmitting. The [Romania lab guide](/romania-lab-guide) sets out what applies; read it before this hardware lab. This course is education, not legal advice.

The first half of this course works almost entirely in <Term id="sub-ghz">Sub-GHz</Term>, the radio spectrum below 1 GHz where cheap short-range remotes live. RFID, NFC, infrared, iButton and GPIO each get their own module later.

<Callout variant="note" title='Why "Sub-GHz"?'>
"Sub" means below. Sub-GHz is the radio spectrum under one gigahertz (1,000 MHz), where cheap short-range remotes live. The Flipper's chip and antenna are designed for **300–348, 387–464 and 779–928 MHz**. Do not memorise those. Module 1 gives you the four numbers that matter.
</Callout>

### Listening and transmitting are different jobs

Receiving puts no energy into the air. Transmitting does, which is why it is the regulated part. Flipper's documentation says the <Term id="region-regulatory">region</Term> is determined during a firmware update, and that only that region's transmit ranges are unlocked. For the European Union the documented ranges are **433.05–434.79 MHz** and **868.15–868.55 MHz**. Flipper's page says "European Union", not a country, so check your national rules too. Try to send outside the allowed ranges and the device refuses.

The region list is a firmware guard rail. It does not replace the conditions set by your national regulator (ANCOM in Romania). You only receive in Week 0 and most of Week 1, and the transmit rules come before you send anything.

### The SD card and your first file format

Captured signals, RFID cards, NFC tags, infrared remotes and apps all land on the microSD card as ordinary files. Without a card, you cannot save captures or update the firmware.

For Sub-GHz, the file you will create over and over is a <Term id="sub-file">.sub file</Term>: a short, human-readable text file that describes one signal. Here is a saved fixed-code remote in the format Flipper's developer documentation gives:

```text
Filetype: Flipper SubGhz Key File
Version: 1
Frequency: 433920000
Preset: FuriHalSubGhzPresetOok650Async
Protocol: Princeton
Bit: 24
Key: 00 00 00 00 00 8C 5A 3B
TE: 400
```

Read it like a label on a jar:

<div class="table-scroll">

| Line | What it means | Units and notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Frequency` | Where the signal lives | Hertz: 433920000 Hz is 433.92 MHz |
| `Preset` | How the radio listens | `Ook650Async` is the AM650 <Term id="preset">preset</Term> |
| `Protocol` | Which remote "language" it speaks | Princeton is a common fixed-code <Term id="protocol">protocol</Term> |
| `Bit` | How long the code is | 24 bits |
| `Key` | The code itself | Hexadecimal bytes |
| `TE` | The basic pulse length | Microseconds |

</div>

Modules 1 to 3 explain each field (frequency, preset, protocol, bit, key, TE), and when Read cannot name a protocol, Read RAW saves raw pulse timings instead of a key (Module 5). Today, notice only that a capture is a small text file you can read.

### What the Frequency Analyzer screen is actually showing you

The analyzer does not draw a spectrum. It runs a two-pass search, over and over:

1. **Coarse pass.** It hops through a list of frequencies, letting the receiver settle for 2 ms on each, and measures signal strength (<Term id="rssi">RSSI</Term>).
2. **Fine pass.** If the strongest reading beats a threshold, it re-scans 300 kHz either side of that frequency in 20 kHz steps and displays the winner. Flipper's documentation gives the threshold as −90 dBm; the official firmware source on its dev branch, checked 13 October 2026, sets it at −93 dBm. This lesson rounds to "about −90 dBm". The 2 ms, 300 kHz and 20 kHz values come from the same source file, so they may change in a later firmware release.

So the number on screen is the frequency with the highest RSSI that cleared the threshold, rounded to a 20 kHz step. Because it hops, a very short burst can fall between visits and be missed, which is why you hold the button down. Flipper's documentation calls the results approximate and says to hold the remote close to the device, mainly so a nearby transmitter you care about beats the others.

#### A worked example in dBm

Signal strength is in <Term id="dbm">dBm</Term>: decibels relative to one milliwatt. Every 10 dB is a factor of ten in power. −90 dBm is one picowatt and −60 dBm is one nanowatt. The difference is 30 dB, so the second signal carries 10 × 10 × 10 = **1,000 times** the power of the first.

In open space, doubling the distance leaves roughly a quarter of the power, about 6 dB less. At 433 MHz the loss is about 25 dB at 1 m and 31 dB at 2 m, so a doorbell button is usually still well above the threshold across a room. Walls, a short burst or the antenna's orientation are more likely reasons for a miss than distance alone.

### Misconceptions to drop now

- **"A blank analyzer means empty air."** It means nothing nearby beat about −90 dBm on the frequencies it scanned. The <Term id="noise-floor">noise floor</Term> is always there.
- **"The analyzer gives the exact frequency."** It gives the strongest 20 kHz step. 433.90 and 433.92 MHz are the same remote.
- **"The analyzer decodes the signal."** It only measures strength. Read and Read RAW name and save signals.
- **"My region list means transmitting is legal."** It is a firmware limit, not a licence.

<Lab kicker="Lab 0.1" title="Watch live radio energy" gear="Flipper, your own fixed-code remote, your own infrared TV remote. The Flipper only receives.">

1. **Predict first.** In your notebook write four predictions: what the analyzer shows with nothing pressed, with your doorbell button a few centimetres away, with an infrared TV remote in the same spot, and at the distance in step 8. A wrong prediction is good evidence; record it as it is.
2. **SD card.** Open <Flip>Settings → Storage</Flip>, check the card is detected and note its size.
3. From the desktop press <kbd>OK</kbd>, then open <Flip>Sub-GHz → Frequency Analyzer</Flip>.
4. **Baseline.** Watch for 20 seconds with nothing pressed. Note whether any frequency appears. A blank screen or a stray result from something else nearby are both normal.
5. **Test press.** Hold your fixed-code remote a few centimetres from the Flipper and press and hold its button. Expect a frequency within a second or two, often near 433.92 MHz in Europe. Record the number.
6. **Release.** Let go and note whether the last result stays on the screen on your firmware. Press again two more times and record each result. Differences of 20 kHz are the step size, not a different remote.
7. **Control.** Press a button on your infrared TV remote in the same spot. It sends light, not Sub-GHz radio, so the analyzer should show nothing from it. If something appears, name the likely other transmitter. Then repeat the baseline once more with no press at all.
8. **Distance.** Move to another room, or 5 to 10 m away with a wall in between, and press again. Record the distance at which the reading stops appearing, or that it never did. Either result is valid.
9. Open <Flip>Sub-GHz → Region Information</Flip> and record what it lists for your region.
10. **Read a file.** Copy the `.sub` example above into your notebook and write the meaning of each line next to it from the table, without looking at the table. If you have a saved capture on the card, compare it.

</Lab>

For most people the first "so this is real" moment is a doorbell button. You press it in your hand and a number appears on a screen a few centimetres away. The decoding in later modules is more useful, but it rarely feels as direct as that first reading.
</Callout>

<Lab kicker="Lab 0.2" title="Build your radio box" gear="A shoebox and the remotes you own">

Good first devices are cheap, fixed-code and yours. Collect a few into one box for the coming weeks:

1. A **wireless doorbell button**, the classic starter.
2. An **RF LED-strip remote**, the small handset for colour-changing lights (skip it if you do not have one).
3. A **ceiling-fan remote**, often simple on, off and speed codes (skip it if you do not have one).
4. An **infrared TV remote** as your standing control.
5. For each one, write the label on the back (many print a frequency such as 433.92 MHz) and check the battery. Predict first: will the measured frequency match the label?
6. **Measure.** Open the Frequency Analyzer, hold each radio remote about 3 cm from the Flipper, press and hold, and record the reading. The infrared remote is the negative case and should give nothing. Compare each reading with its label.

Leave remotes for cars, gates, garages and alarms out of the box. They usually use <Term id="rolling-code">rolling codes</Term> and are tied to systems other people depend on. Module 5 teaches rolling codes with a simulator instead.

</Lab>

<Win>every radio remote in the box has a battery check, a label frequency and a measured frequency, the infrared remote shows no reading, and you have written down any remote whose reading differs from its label by more than 20 kHz.</Win>

<Callout variant="tip" title="Batteries matter">
A weak battery makes a remote unreliable: it may send a short or garbled burst, or nothing. If a remote shows nothing at a few centimetres, try a fresh cell before you blame the Flipper.
</Callout>
