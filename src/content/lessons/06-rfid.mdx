## Outcome

You can read, save and emulate an unenrolled 125 kHz training tag on the Flipper, and explain why it only works a few centimetres from the coil.

By the end you can:

- explain how a tag with no battery is powered by the reader and answers by load modulation;
- read the Flipper's result screen and say which lines come from the tag and which are the Flipper's interpretation;
- measure your read distance flat and edge-on and explain the difference.

## Understand

### A different kind of radio

Low-frequency <Term id="rfid">RFID</Term> at 125 kHz uses the same verbs on the Flipper as <Term id="sub-ghz">Sub-GHz</Term> (read, save, emulate) on different physics. A Sub-GHz remote has a battery and launches a wave that keeps travelling on its own (far-field radio). A 125 kHz tag has no battery and does nothing until a reader's coil powers it through a shared magnetic field, like a loosely coupled transformer. "Which app do I open?" starts with which of these three you are holding.

<div class="table-scroll">

| | Sub-GHz | 125 kHz RFID (LF) | NFC (HF) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Frequency | 315, 433.92, 868 MHz and others | 125 kHz | 13.56 MHz |
| Wavelength | about 0.35 to 1 m | 2,398 m | 22.1 m |
| Typical range | metres to tens of metres | a few centimetres | a few centimetres |
| Power | the transmitter has a battery | passive tag, powered by the reader | passive tag, powered by the reader |
| Mechanism | far-field radio wave | near-field magnetic coupling | near-field magnetic coupling |
| Flipper app | Sub-GHz | 125 kHz RFID | NFC |

</div>

### Why the range is centimetres

Run 125 kHz through the formula from Module 1: `λ = 299,792,458 m/s ÷ 125,000 Hz = 2,398 m`. An antenna radiates well when it is a decent fraction of a wavelength, and nobody puts a kilometre of wire in a key fob. So neither side radiates: the reader drives current through a small coil and the tag's coil sits inside its magnetic field. Microchip's antenna note AN710 calls this kind of coil a magnetic dipole and gives the field on the coil's axis:

`B = μ0 · N · I · a² ÷ (2 · (a² + r²)^(3/2))`

where `a` is the coil radius, `r` the distance along the axis, `N` the turns and `I` the current. Once `r` is several times `a`, the field falls with `1/r³`, so each doubling of distance keeps about an eighth. Close to the coil it falls more slowly: the figure below goes from 35% at 2 cm to 9% at 4 cm, a factor of four.

A larger reader coil reaches further, so the short range belongs to small coils. AN710 also gives the tag's induced voltage as proportional to `cos α`, where `α` is the angle between the field and the tag coil's axis. On the coil's axis, an edge-on tag has `α` near 90° and the voltage falls toward zero. Off the axis the field has a radial part, so an edge-on tag need not drop to zero there; Lab 6.2 measures it centred.

### How a tag with no battery answers

<Scene3D name="nearfield" opts={{ kind: 'fob' }} label="3D animation: a reader coil couples with a key fob coil through a near field; out of range, the fob has no power." />

The reader drives its coil at 125 kHz. Inside the field, the tag's coil picks up a voltage, the chip rectifies it and runs on it, and it takes its clock from the same 125 kHz. To answer, the tag switches a load across its own coil on and off. Microchip's ATA5577C datasheet calls this load modulation: a resistive load across the coil terminals damps the field. The reader sees the tiny changes in its own coil and turns them back into bits. The tag never transmits on its own.

### What is on the tag

An access tag stores a short number; the door controller looks it up in its own list. Flipper's reading guide lists 24 protocols the app decodes. Three are worth knowing by structure.

#### EM4100: a 64-bit frame around 40 bits of ID

In firmware 1.4.3's EM4100 decoder the frame is 64 bits: nine 1s as a header, then ten rows of four data bits each followed by an even parity bit (a bit chosen so the row holds an even number of 1s), then four column parity bits and a 0 stop bit. That is 9 + 50 + 4 + 1 = 64.

The data cannot imitate the header. A row ends in at most four 1s (`0111` then parity `1`) and starts with at most four (`1111` then `0`), so two rows back to back give at most eight, and the stop bit is always 0.

**Worked example: how long is one read?** At RF/64 each bit lasts 64 carrier cycles: `125,000 ÷ 64 = 1,953 bit/s`, so one 64-bit frame takes `64 ÷ 1,953 = 32.8 ms`. When Flipper's docs say a read can take up to 10 seconds for some cards, the time goes on finding the right demodulator and a clean signal, not on the frame itself.

#### H10301: facility code and card number

HID describes H10301 as an open 26-bit format: bit 1 is even parity over bits 2 to 13, bits 2 to 9 are an 8-bit facility code (up to 255), bits 10 to 25 are a 16-bit card number (up to 65,535), and bit 26 is odd parity over bits 14 to 25. The facility code names the site or customer, the card number the person. A controller set for facility 42 refuses a valid card from facility 7.

None of these formats is encrypted. The tag recites its number to anything that powers it, and the security lives in the controller's list; Flipper's overview says LF cards do not usually provide high levels of security.

### What the screen is actually showing you

Open <Flip>125 kHz RFID → Read</Flip>. The right side of the screen alternates between `Reading 1/2` with `ASK` and `Reading 2/2` with `PSK`, and the bottom says `Don't move card`. The two passes use different demodulators: in firmware 1.4.3, EM4100 and FSK formats such as H10301 decode in the ASK pass, and Indala 26 needs the PSK pass. Flipper's guide says the switch happens every three seconds (the 1.4.3 source uses a 2-second window plus settling time). To force one pass, use <Flip>Extra Actions → Read ASK (Animal, Ordinary Card)</Flip> or <Flip>Extra Actions → Read PSK (Indala)</Flip>.

After a read the screen shows the manufacturer and protocol (`EM-Micro EM4100`), a `Hex:` line with the decoded bytes (`01 23 45 67 89`), and the Flipper's interpretation of those bytes. For EM4100, firmware 1.4.3 prints `FC: 069` and `Card: 26505 (RF/64)`: it takes the third byte (`0x45` = 69) as a facility code and the last two (`0x6789` = 26,505) as a card number. That split is a display convention borrowed from formats like H10301. The chip holds one flat 40-bit number, so the hex line is the tag and the FC and Card lines are the Flipper's reading of it.

<Flip>More</Flip> offers <Flip>Save</Flip>, <Flip>Emulate</Flip> and <Flip>Write</Flip>. A saved key is a small `.rfid` text file on the microSD card:

```
Filetype: Flipper RFID key
Version: 1
Key type: EM4100
Data: 01 23 45 67 89
```

From <Flip>125 kHz RFID → Saved</Flip>, a key offers Emulate, Write, Edit, Rename, Delete and Info. Emulate shows `Emulating` with the key's name and blinks the LED magenta until you press Back. Write shows `Writing`, then reads the tag back to check it. If that keeps failing you see `Still Trying to Write...` with "Make sure this card is writable and not protected".

<Callout variant="warn" title='"Successfully written" is weaker than it sounds'>
Write needs a T5577, which can be password-protected; ordinary 125 kHz tags are read-only. Do not write to cards of unknown origin. Flipper's write guide warns that the success message can appear on a read-only card that already holds the same data, because the check is a read-back. Use a blank and a different ID to prove a real write.
</Callout>

### The T5577: one chip, many formats

Training tags sold as T5577 use a chip Microchip documents as the ATA5577C. Its datasheet lists 363 bits of EEPROM in 11 blocks of 33 bits (32 data bits plus a lock bit), including a 32-bit password block, and a configuration register that sets the data rate (RF/2 to RF/128) and the coding: Manchester, biphase, FSK, PSK or NRZ. Set those to match EM4100, fill the data blocks with an EM4100 frame, and a reader cannot tell the difference. A factory blank has no such configuration, so it reads as nothing until you write one. The 1.4.3 source does this in three blocks: block 0 sets Manchester at RF/64, blocks 1 and 2 hold the 64-bit frame.

### Misconceptions

- **"A better antenna would read my fob from across the room."** Bigger coils reach further, but beyond a few coil radii the field still loses about seven eighths per doubling of distance.
- **"125 kHz tags are encrypted."** EM4100 and H10301 carry a plain number with parity. The security is in the controller's list.
- **"The FC on an EM4100 read is stored on the tag."** It is the Flipper's display split of the last three bytes.
- **"No read means a dead tag."** It can mean a bad orientation, a wait too short for the PSK pass, a 13.56 MHz card (try the NFC app) or a T5577 that holds no ID yet.

2. Open <Flip>125 kHz RFID → Read</Flip>. Lay a training tag flat on the back, centred on the antenna, and hold still until the protocol appears. Copy the protocol and `Hex:` lines into your notebook. No read after several passes may mean a factory blank; use the other tag, or see step 8.
3. Build spacers from your stack, 5 mm per step, and start at 30 mm. Press <Flip>Retry</Flip>, hold the tag flat and centred on the stack, and wait through one ASK and one PSK pass. Remove a spacer and repeat down to contact. Record the greatest height that reads on two tries out of two.
4. Repeat with the tag edge-on (its face at 90° to the back, still centred over the antenna), held between two fingertips. Record the greatest height that reads, or "no read at contact".
5. **Control:** hold your NFC tag flat and centred at contact and again at your flat read height, through two full ASK and PSK cycles each. Record what the screen shows.
6. Read the training tag again, then <Flip>More → Save</Flip> with a clear name such as `training-tag-01`.
7. Open <Flip>Saved</Flip>, select it and choose <Flip>Emulate</Flip>. With a desk reader, hold the Flipper's back to it and check it shows the same ID. Press Back to stop.
8. **Optional write:** <Flip>Saved → training-tag-01 → Edit</Flip>, change one byte, save it under a new name, then Write it to your second T5577. Read it and confirm it shows the edited ID, not the original. A bought blank can carry a password and fail to write.

</Lab>

Expected observation: flat, a few centimetres at most; edge-on and centred, much shorter, possibly no read. Your numbers depend on the tag's coil, so the comparison matters more than the figure. The NFC tag never reads in this app.
