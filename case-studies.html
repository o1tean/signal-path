Case studies · real beginner mysteries

"Wait, why did that happen?"

Almost everyone new to the Flipper hits the same handful of confusing moments. None of them are bugs. Each one is a piece of RF explaining itself — once you know what you're looking at.

The Nice gate: copies once, then barely works

Symptom
You capture your Nice gate remote. The replay opens the gate the first time. Then it stops working. Occasionally, seemingly at random, it catches again — but mostly it does nothing.
What's happening
Your gate uses a rolling code (the Nice FloR-S family). Every press is a one-time password.
What to do
Stop replaying, and enroll a genuine remote through the receiver's pairing procedure instead.

This is the single most common beginner mystery, and it's the cleanest demonstration of rolling codes there is — so it's worth slowing down on. When you press a rolling-code remote, it doesn't send a fixed message. It sends a fresh number, derived from a secret it shares with the receiver and a counter that ticks up by one on every press. The receiver keeps its own copy of that counter, and it enforces a simple rule: only accept a code whose counter is ahead of the last one I honoured, and refuse anything I've already seen.

Now the whole story falls out of that one rule:

  • Why the first replay works: you captured, say, counter #500. If the receiver last honoured #499, your #500 is a valid next step — so the gate opens, and the receiver advances its counter to 500.
  • Why it then fails: your recording is still #500. The receiver has already moved past it. To the gate, #500 is now old news, so it's rejected. Every subsequent replay of the same capture is stale by definition.
  • Why it occasionally catches again: receivers accept a small window of future counters — often a few hundred ahead — so a remote that was pressed a few times out of range still works when you get back. If real-remote presses and your replays happen to line up inside that window, a stale-looking code can occasionally slip through. It's coincidence, not a fix.
i
This is the security feature working A rolling-code replay failing isn't the Flipper being weak or you doing it wrong. It's the exact behaviour the system was designed to produce: yesterday's recording is worthless. Understanding why is the real skill here.
!
Replaying can desync your real remote Push enough stale or out-of-order codes at the receiver and you can drag its counter window out of step with your genuine remote, so the real one stops working too until it's re-paired. Another good reason to stop replaying and pair properly.

The supported way to add a working remote to a gate you own is the one the manufacturer documents: put the receiver into its pairing/learn mode and enroll a genuine remote. That's not a consolation prize — it's the correct answer, and it's how these systems are meant to grow. Want to watch the counter logic play out safely? The car fob that won't clone

Symptom
You try to copy your car key fob so you have a spare. The Flipper reads something, but the copy never unlocks the car.
What's happening
Modern car fobs are rolling code too, so the same one-time-password logic applies — plus a car isn't designed to be taught a new remote by radio at all.
What to do
Add a spare the way cars actually do it: programming, not cloning.

It's tempting to think of a spare remote as a photocopy, but that's not how cars work. The fob and the car share a secret that was paired at the factory or by a dealer, and — like the gate above — each press is a throwaway code, so a recording can't stand in for the real thing. Cloning the radio signal was never the mechanism for adding a car remote.

The real path to a spare is enrolling a new fob into the car, which depends on the make and model. Some cars have an owner-accessible programming sequence in the manual; many require dealer or locksmith tooling that talks to the car's computer directly. Your owner's manual (or the dealer) is the authority. The Flipper is a superb tool for understanding what your fob transmits; it isn't a key-cutting machine.

The garage remote that copied perfectly — and still works

Symptom
You capture an older garage-door remote, replay it, and it opens the door every single time. Unlike the gate, it never stops.
What's happening
This remote is fixed code same every press. There's no counter to move past.
What to do
Nothing's wrong — enjoy it, and understand what it means.

Plenty of older openers (and cheap gate remotes) send the identical message on every press — often a simple protocol like Princeton with a 24-bit key. Because the message never changes, a recording of it is just as valid tomorrow as today. That's exactly why fixed-code devices are the perfect first capture in Module 3: they behave predictably and teach the whole capture-save-replay loop on your own hardware.

✓
The flip side of "it always works" Fixed code is convenient and also the weaker design — anything that can hear it can repeat it. If a device you own protects something that matters, its fixed-code behaviour is a nudge to consider a rolling-code upgrade. That's you improving your own security, which is always fair game.

Frequency Analyzer says 433.92, but Read shows nothing

Symptom
The Frequency Analyzer clearly locks onto your remote at 433.92 MHz. But when you switch to Read and press the button, nothing decodes.
What's happening
You have the right frequency but the wrong modulation, or a protocol the decoder doesn't recognise.
What to do
Change the preset; if that fails, capture with Read RAW.

Finding the frequency is only half the job. Read has to decode the signal, and that needs the right modulation preset and a protocol grammar it knows. If your remote speaks FSK and you're listening on an AM preset (or vice versa), the energy is there but the message is gibberish — like tuning to the correct station in the wrong language. Work through it in order:

TryWhy
Confirm the frequency, then set AM650The most common preset; fits most fixed-code remotes.
Try AM270, then FM238 / FM476You may be on the wrong modulation family entirely.
Switch to Read RAWCaptures the pulses even when no decoder recognises the protocol.

A successful RAW capture that never decodes into a named protocol is a totally normal outcome — it just means the Flipper doesn't have that grammar built in, not that you failed. The presets reference lists what each one is for, and Module 9 turns this into a full diagnostic flow.

I read my office badge fine, but the clone won't open the door

Symptom
The Flipper reads your work or hotel badge and shows an ID. You write it to a blank, but the copy doesn't open the door.
What's happening
The door system checks more than the number you can read — and there's a policy layer on top of the technical one.
What to do
Treat access credentials as off-limits unless you administer the system.

Reading an ID off a badge isn't the same as reproducing everything the reader validates. Many access systems layer on protections a simple clone can't satisfy: cryptographic challenge-response, rolling counters, anti-cloning checks, or a back-end that flags the same credential appearing in two places. So a copy that reads perfectly can still fail at the door — by design.

!
The bigger stop here is policy, not tech Your work or building badge belongs to a system someone else administers. Copying it usually breaks the acceptable-use agreement you signed, even where it isn't a crime. Keep your RFID and NFC practice on tags you bought to experiment with — see the ground rules on access badges. Learn the tech on your own fobs.
