Authorized security research: test the claim

Study how replay, credential copying, relay, and interference attacks differ, then test one bounded security claim on a training system.

2–3 sessionsStandalone training receiver and tags

Understand it

Separate the attacker’s capabilities

Replay repeats an observed message. A simple fixed-code receiver may accept it because the message has no freshness check. A changing encrypted counter or a fresh challenge can let a receiver reject old messages. Encryption alone does not automatically provide freshness.

Credential copying reproduces the data a reader relies on. A UID-only training reader may accept two devices with the same ID. A cryptographic card-reader exchange can require a secret that a UID read does not reveal. “NFC,” “RFID,” and “encrypted” are categories, not verdicts about security.

Relay forwards a live exchange between legitimate endpoints. It differs from replay because the forwarded response may be fresh. Authentication that proves possession of a key does not necessarily prove physical distance; timing and proximity defenses address a different property.

Interference-assisted capture attempts to prevent a legitimate receiver accepting a message while an adversary records it. That changes whether a captured value is still fresh. Jamming also disrupts other users of the spectrum: owning the test receiver does not provide a general right to jam. Study this mechanism in the browser model; over-the-air interference needs a separately assessed, legally compliant RF test facility.

Input injection abuses a trusted input path, such as USB HID. The text-only lab in Module 13 demonstrates the trust boundary without a destructive payload. Fuzzing feeds varied or malformed inputs to find incorrect handling; start with an offline parser or a disposable app you wrote.

What the Flipper actually establishes

A protocol name is a decoder result. A read UID is an identifier. An accepted replay is evidence about that receiver, configuration, and message. None of these alone proves that an entire product family is compromised. The rolling-code simulator omits real cryptography and vendor-specific recovery logic; use it to test freshness reasoning, not to claim an exploit against a manufacturer.

Lab: build the evidence

  1. Write the claim: “My isolated, fixed-code training receiver accepts a repeated authorized command” or “My UID-only bench reader treats a copied training ID as the same tag.” Define the target, permission, permitted action, and recovery first.
  2. For RF, complete the Romanian radio checks and use a non-security device such as a lab chime. For the tag experiment, use unenrolled practice tags and a standalone reader with no building connection. If you lack that setup, use the synthetic rolling-code simulator and label the result simulated.
  3. Establish a positive control using the original remote or tag. Record the expected harmless outcome and the receiver configuration.
  4. Follow Module 4’s fixed-code replay or Module 6’s training-tag write/emulate procedure once. Record whether the reader accepts the replayed message or copied identifier.
  5. Use a negative control: a different unenrolled training tag, or a non-matching saved button on the same lab remote. Avoid guessing live credentials or cycling through code spaces. A rejected control makes the acceptance result easier to interpret.
  6. Compare your finding with the rolling-code model: accept a fresh value, then replay the consumed value. Explain what freshness adds and why adding encryption without replay protection is a different claim.
  7. Write a mitigation recommendation tied to your result, such as authenticated messages with freshness checks or a supported cryptographic credential system. Remove test enrollments and restore the lab configuration.

Show that you can do it

  • A threat model identifying access, capability, target, and security property.
  • A positive control, a bounded test, and a negative control on authorized equipment or an explicitly labelled simulation.
  • A precise finding, alternative explanations, mitigation, and cleanup record.
