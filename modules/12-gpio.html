GPIO: signals you can touch

Build a low-current LED circuit and learn to check a pinout before connecting hardware.

60–90 minBreadboard, LED, 1 kΩ resistor, jumpers

Understand it

GPIO pins are electrical connections. A logic output represents a low or high voltage; it is not a general-purpose power supply. Follow the current Flipper pinout and use 3.3 V logic for these exercises. The separate 5 V supply pin does not mean an arbitrary signal pin is safe for 5 V.

An LED needs a series resistor. With a 3.3 V output, a red LED of approximately 2 V forward voltage, and a 1 kΩ resistor, the estimated current is (3.3 − 2) / 1000 ≈ 1.3 mA. Forward voltage varies; this is a conservative teaching circuit, not a device measurement.

UART, SPI and I²C

UART exchanges asynchronous serial data: endpoints must agree on baud rate and framing, and TX connects to the other end’s RX. SPI uses a clock and a chip-select signal. I²C shares clock and data lines with pull-ups and device addresses. All require compatible electrical levels and a common reference. A connector’s shape is not enough to identify its voltage.

Lab: build the evidence

  1. Disconnect USB and turn off the Flipper before wiring. Open the official pinout. Identify PA7 (pin 2) and GND (pin 8) by label and orientation, not by counting from memory.
  2. On the breadboard connect PA7 → 1 kΩ resistor → LED anode; connect LED cathode → GND. Check that no supply pins are shorted. If you cannot identify LED polarity, use its datasheet first.
  3. Power the Flipper. Open GPIO → GPIO Manual Control, select PA7, and use the on-screen control to change its output. Observe the LED. Do not connect this circuit to a mains-powered board.
  4. If it does not light, power down before checking polarity, resistor value, continuity, and the selected pin. Change one thing at a time.
  5. Set the output inactive, switch off, and disconnect. Draw the circuit with pin names, resistor value, and expected current.
  6. For a follow-up, read the USB–UART bridge documentation and draw the TX/RX/GND connections to a documented 3.3 V training board. Verify its pinout and power arrangement before a future connection.

Show that you can do it

  • An annotated circuit diagram and estimated LED current.
  • An observed high/low result with a controlled troubleshooting step.
  • A clear explanation of why unknown voltages, RS-232 levels, and mains boards are excluded.

Record the result in your learning notebook. Repeat the explanation tomorrow without looking at this page.
