Capstone: your signal atlas

No new tricks this week. Instead, you turn the whole course loose on the real world: every remote, fob and card you own, identified and catalogued in one place.

⏱ 60 min+ Gear: Flipper + every RF thing in your home

Goal

A course teaches you nothing until you can use it without the course. So this module has no fresh material to memorise. The task is simple to say and satisfying to do: walk through your home, and for every radio-frequency device you own, work out what it is and write it down. Frequency, modulation, protocol, fixed or rolling, and one honest sentence about whether a replay would work.

By the end you will have a personal signal atlas — a catalogue of the invisible RF around you that you built yourself, from scratch, with a tool that fits in your pocket. That atlas is the proof. If you can classify a remote you've never seen before using the same steps every time, you have actually learned this.

★
Why this is the real examAnyone can follow a lab with the answer written at the bottom. The skill is walking up to an unknown device and, in a couple of minutes, saying "868 MHz, FSK, rolling — a replay won't work, and here's why." That's what you're demonstrating today, one device at a time.

The workflow you now own

Everything from Modules 1 through 9 collapses into one repeatable routine. Point it at any unknown Sub-GHz remote and it always runs the same way:

  1. Identify the frequency. Sub-GHz → Frequency Analyzer, remote held close, button held down. Read the number: usually 315, 433.92, 868 or 915 MHz. No number? It may be too weak, or on a frequency outside the analyzer's default list.
  2. Identify the modulation. Open Sub-GHz → Read on that frequency and sweep the presets — AM650 AM270 FM238 FM476 — until it decodes. The winning preset tells you AM/OOK vs FM/FSK.
  3. Decode, or go raw. If Read names a protocol, record it. If it stays blank but the analyzer saw a peak, capture with Sub-GHz → Read RAW — the signal is real, the protocol just isn't one the Flipper knows.
  4. Fixed or rolling? Press it several times on the Read screen. A stable key that never changes is fixed. A Cnt that climbs by one each press is rolling.
  5. Save and label. Give it a clear name you'll recognise in a year — garage-main-433 beats Sub_042. Save a real capture for every fixed-code device.
  6. Note whether replay would work, and why. Fixed → yes, to your own gear. Rolling → no, the counter defeats it. Raw fixed-code → usually yes. Write the reason, not just the yes/no.

RFID and NFC follow the same spirit even though the tools differ: for a 125 kHz fob you'll use the Flipper's RFID reader (EM4100, HID Prox, Indala) and note whether it's a writable T5577 blank; for a 13.56 MHz card you'll use NFC (Mifare, NTAG), knowing a bank card only ever gives up limited public data. Same routine: identify, classify, label, and be honest about what can and can't be copied.

Lab: catalog your home

Lab 10.1

The capstone project — your complete signal atlas

Gear: your Flipper, and every RF device you own — Sub-GHz remotes (garage, gate, car, ceiling fan, cheap plug sockets, doorbell, weather station), 125 kHz fobs, and 13.56 MHz cards.

  1. Round them up. Walk every room and gather everything with a button or a tap-to-work card. You'll find more than you expect — remotes hide in drawers.
  2. Run the workflow on each Sub-GHz device. Frequency (Analyzer) → modulation (sweep the four presets) → Read or Read RAW → fixed vs rolling (watch for Cnt).
  3. Record five things per device in the atlas above: frequency, modulation, protocol (or "raw"), fixed vs rolling, and one line on why replay would or wouldn't work.
  4. Save a labeled capture for every fixed-code device. Clear names you'll still understand in a year. Skip captures for rolling devices — you know they won't replay, and the file would only mislead future-you.
  5. Add your RFID and NFC items too. Note the fob type (EM4100, HID Prox, Indala) and whether it's a writable T5577, and the card type (Mifare, NTAG) for 13.56 MHz.
  6. Read your atlas back. Count how many are rolling vs fixed. That ratio is a real snapshot of how the security around you actually works.
✓You've got it when: every RF device you own has a row — frequency, modulation, protocol, fixed/rolling, and a one-line reason replay would or wouldn't work — and you saved a labeled capture for each fixed-code device. If you can do that unaided, you've met the whole course.

Where to go next

You've reached the edge of this course, not the edge of the subject. RF is deep, and the Flipper only touches part of it. A few honest directions, kept high-level — the live links live on the reference page so they stay current:

Read the real docs

  • Official Flipper documentation — the authoritative source for what each menu and file format actually does.
  • Momentum documentation — the custom presets, extended frequencies and settings this course used, explained by the people who built them.
  • The community — forums, subreddits and chats where people post captures, protocol notes and honest "here's what didn't work" stories.

Find all of these on the reference page.

Pick a next skill

  • Sub-GHz protocol internals — how a protocol like Princeton actually encodes bits in pulse timing, and how decoders are written.
  • NFC deep dive — the 13.56 MHz world is a course of its own: sectors, keys, and what's readable versus protected.
  • GPIO & hardware — wire sensors and modules to the Flipper's pins and read the physical world directly.
  • Building .fap apps — write your own Flipper application once you want a tool that doesn't exist yet.
