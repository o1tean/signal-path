What the Flipper actually is

The Flipper Zero is a multi-tool for the wireless world. Think of a Swiss Army knife where each blade is a different way of talking to everyday electronics. Most of those "blades" are separate little radios and interfaces, each tuned to a different technology:

ToolWhat it talks toEveryday examples
Sub-GHz radio our focusLow-power radios below 1 GHz, via a TI CC1101 chipGarage remotes, doorbells, TPMS, weather stations, key fobs
125 kHz RFIDLow-frequency access cards (unpowered coils)Older building fobs, gym tags, pet chips
13.56 MHz NFCHigh-frequency smartcards (ISO 14443)Transit cards, hotel keys, contactless payments
InfraredLine-of-sight IR lightTVs, air-conditioners, projectors
iButton / 1-WireContact "touch" keysIntercom keys, some door contacts
GPIO pinsDirect wires to other electronicsSensors, dev boards, hardware hacking

That is a lot of doors. Here is the good news: the first half of this course focuses on Sub-GHz, then expands to other interfaces. Signal Path is almost entirely about the Sub-GHz radio — the part that listens to and repeats the little radio remotes scattered around your home. RFID and NFC get one module each near the end so you know they exist and how they differ, but Sub-GHz is where a beginner learns the most, fastest, with the least risk.

?
Why "Sub-GHz"?"Sub" means "below." Sub-GHz means "below one gigahertz" — the stretch of radio spectrum under 1,000 MHz where cheap, short-range remotes live. The Flipper's radio chip physically covers 300–348, 387–464, and 779–928 MHz. Do not memorize those; you will meet the four numbers that matter in Module 1.

One more reframe that helps enormously: listening is not the same as transmitting. Receiving radio is passive and, not a blanket permission to capture private communications. Transmitting is the part that is regulated, because you are now putting energy into the air that can reach other people's devices. For all of Week 0 and most of Week 1, you are only ever listening. We will make the transmit rules crystal clear before you send a single signal.

The SD card & your first file format

Slide a microSD card into the Flipper and it becomes the device's filing cabinet. Almost everything you save — captured signals, RFID cards, NFC tags, infrared remotes, downloaded apps — lands on that card as ordinary files. No card, no saving.

For Sub-GHz, the file you will create over and over is a .sub file: a small, human-readable text file describing one captured signal. You do not need to understand its guts today, but a peek now makes Module 3 far less mysterious. A saved fixed-code remote looks roughly like this:

Filetype: Flipper SubGhz Key File
Version: 1
Frequency: 433920000
Preset: FuriHalSubGhzPresetOok650Async
Protocol: Princeton
Bit: 24
Key: 00 00 00 00 00 8C 5A 3B
TE: 400

Read it like a label on a jar: Frequency is where the signal lives (433.92 MHz here), Preset is how to listen (a modulation setting), Protocol is which remote "language" it speaks, and Key is the actual code that was sent. Every one of those words gets its own module. Today, just notice that a captured radio signal is, in the end, a tiny text file you can open and read.

Getting around: buttons and menus

Five controls do everything. Learn these and you can drive the whole device blind.

D-pad
The four-way ring: Up / Down move through a list, Left / Right change values or move between screens.
OK
The center button. Select / confirm / go in. When in doubt, press OK.
Back
Steps out one level. Hold Back to jump straight to the desktop (the dolphin home screen) from anywhere.

From the desktop, pressing OK (or the appropriate direction) opens the main menu: a scrolling list of apps, one per tool — Sub-GHz, 125 kHz RFID, NFC, Infrared, GPIO, iButton, plus Apps and Settings. Momentum may reorder this list or add entries, so scroll the whole thing once to see what you have rather than trusting a fixed position.

Lab 0: the tour

Two small missions: confirm the radio breathes, and gather your props for the weeks ahead.

Lab 0.1

Watch live radio energy

Gear: Flipper only — no capture, no transmit

  1. From the desktop, open the main menu and select Sub-GHz.
  2. Choose Sub-GHz → Frequency Analyzer. It checks configured frequencies and reports a sufficiently strong nearby signal. It is not a continuous calibrated spectrum display.
  3. Observe the display before your test press. It may show no detected frequency; that does not mean the environment contains no radio energy.
  4. Use your authorized, locally compliant training remote — a wireless chime button is suitable — hold it a few centimeters from the Flipper, and press it. Watch the reading jump.
  5. Release the button and compare what the analyzer reports. Record the displayed frequency and the limits of this observation.
Lab 0.2

Round up your props

Gear: a shoebox and a wander around the house

Good beginner devices are cheap, fixed-code, and yours. Collect a few of these into one "radio box":

Great first targets usually fixed-code

  • Wireless doorbell button — the classic, near-perfect starter.
  • RF LED-strip remote — the little handset for color-changing lights.
  • Ceiling-fan remote — often plain on/off/speed codes.
  • Old garage remote from a pre-2000s opener you own.

Interesting, but expect surprises often rolling

  • Car key fob — modern ones use rolling codes and won't simply replay. Great to observe, not to copy.
  • Newer gate/garage remotes — many are rolling too.

Rolling-code devices are the star of Module 5. Keep them in the box; you'll understand exactly why they behave "weirdly" soon.

!
Batteries matterA remote with a dying battery transmits weakly and may barely register. If a device shows nothing on Test, a fresh coin cell is often the fix before you blame the Flipper.
✓You've got it when: pressing a remote makes the Test reading jump above the resting noise floor, and drop back when you let go — and you have a small box of your own remotes ready for Module 1. You just watched invisible radio become a number on a screen. That's the whole job.
