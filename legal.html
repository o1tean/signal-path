Ground rules · read before Module 4

The rules aren't the boring part.

They're the part that keeps this hobby fun, legal, and something you can be proud of. This page is warm but serious. Read it once, and you'll know exactly where the bright line is — and why it's there.

The one rule

Everything on this page collapses to a single sentence, so let's put it first:

!
Own devices, own space Only capture and replay signals from devices you own, or have explicit permission to test, in a space you control. Never transmit toward property that isn't yours.

That's it. If you can honestly say "this is my remote, my gate, my fob, in my home or lab, and I'm the only person affected," you're on solid ground for the whole course. Almost every rule below is just that sentence applied to a specific situation. The moment a signal you send could reach someone else's car, someone else's gate, or a shared radio band that other people are relying on, you've crossed out of "learning on my own gear" and into "affecting other people" — and that's where law and ethics both kick in.

Listening vs transmitting

The most useful mental model in this whole hobby is that receiving and transmitting are two completely different activities with completely different risk profiles.

Receiving is passive. When you listen — Frequency Analyzer, Read, Read RAW — your Flipper radiates essentially nothing. You're observing energy that's already in the air. In most places, quietly listening to radio for your own learning is low-risk, the same way noticing which radio stations exist isn't the same as broadcasting. It's how you'll spend the large majority of this course, and it's where you can be curious without much worry.

Transmitting is regulated. The instant you send your own signal, you're sharing a finite, public resource — the radio spectrum — with everyone around you. That's why every country has a regulator that sets the rules for who may transmit, on which frequencies, at what power. The licence-free ISM bands (Industrial, Scientific, Medical) let small devices transmit without a personal licence, but only if they stay polite: low power, and often a duty-cycle limit (a cap on how much of the time you're allowed to actually be transmitting, so no one hogs the channel). Those limits are baked into the certified remotes you own; when you transmit, you're expected to respect the same spirit.

Radio is governed locally, so the name on the rulebook changes with your address:

United States
FCC (Federal Communications Commission) — unlicensed devices fall under Part 15.
United Kingdom
Ofcom.
European Union
CEPT for spectrum allocation; ETSI writes the harmonised standards (e.g. for short-range devices).
Canada
ISED (Innovation, Science and Economic Development).
Australia
ACMA (Australian Communications and Media Authority).
Elsewhere
Nearly every country has an equivalent. If you travel with the device, the local rules travel too.

Green light vs hard stop

Here's the practical version — a clear picture of what this hobby looks like when it's healthy, and the handful of things that turn it into something harmful or illegal.

Green light Go learn

  • Read and analyse any signal you like — receiving is how you learn.
  • Save your own remotes, fobs and sensors to a personal library of .sub files.
  • Replay a fixed-code signal to your own device — open your own garage from a capture.
  • Build a home library of everything you own, and catalogue it (that's the capstone).
  • Buy cheap test remotes and fobs specifically to experiment on. Blank T5577 cards and dollar-store remotes are perfect practice targets.

Hard stop Never do this

  • Transmitting to other people's cars, gates, garages, or door systems.
  • Jamming, flooding or spamming a band — deliberately drowning out other devices.
  • Defeating rolling security on property you don't own, by any means.
  • Blindly using Momentum's region unlocks to transmit on frequencies that are illegal where you are.
  • Using "brute-force" or jammer community apps against others. They exist; using them on other people's devices is illegal and harmful, and this course gives no steps for it.
!
The honest test Before you press transmit, ask: "Who, other than me, could this signal reach or affect?" If the answer is "no one — it's my device in my space," go ahead. If it's anyone else, stop.

Rolling codes & why replay isn't a "hack"

A lot of newcomers arrive expecting the Flipper to be a skeleton key. It isn't, and the reason is worth understanding rather than resenting. Modern gates, garages and car fobs use rolling code: the remote and receiver share a secret and a counter, so every button press produces a brand-new, one-time message. Once the receiver has seen a given code, it won't accept it again.

That's why capturing a rolling-code remote and replaying it usually does nothing — the code you recorded is already spent. This is not a puzzle to be solved; it's the security feature working exactly as designed. Understanding why replay fails — counters, acceptance windows, why a copy sometimes works once and then desyncs a real remote — is genuinely educational, and you'll explore it hands-on in Module 5 and in the case studies.

What this course will not do is walk you through defeating that security on hardware you don't own. Techniques that attack rolling code by interfering with a live transmission are illegal to use against other people, and we describe them only far enough to explain why rolling code exists — never as instructions. The right way to add a working remote to a gate you own is to enroll a genuine remote through the receiver's own pairing procedure, which the manufacturer documents. That's not a workaround; it's the supported answer.

A note on access badges, RFID/NFC and workplace policy

Modules 6 and 7 cover 125 kHz RFID and 13.56 MHz NFC — the tech behind building fobs and contactless cards. Reading and emulating your own gym fob or apartment tag is a great way to learn. But access credentials sit on top of a second, non-technical layer: policy.

Your work or building badge belongs to a system that someone else administers, and copying it — even "just to see if it works" — usually violates the acceptable-use agreement you signed, even where it isn't a crime. Access systems also frequently check more than the number the Flipper can read: rolling counters, cryptographic challenges, or a back-end that flags a card seen in two places at once. So a clone that reads perfectly may still not open the door, by design. Treat badges as off-limits unless you administer the system or have written permission, and keep your practice on tags you bought to experiment with.

!
Permission is a document, not a vibe "I'm sure it's fine" isn't permission. For anything you don't personally own, permission means an explicit yes from the person who does — ideally in writing.

The responsible-disclosure mindset

As you get better, you'll eventually notice something that looks weak — a gate that still uses fixed code, a sensor that leaks more than it should. What you do next is what separates a hobbyist from a nuisance.

The responsible move is report, don't exploit. If it's your own device, you can simply fix or replace it. If it's a product or a system that affects other people, the ethical path is to tell the party who can fix it — the manufacturer, the vendor, the building manager — privately, give them reasonable time, and never use the weakness against anyone in the meantime. You don't need to publish a proof-of-concept to be taken seriously, and you should never test a finding on equipment you don't own. Curiosity that protects people is a good look; curiosity that harms them isn't curiosity any more.

This course's stance

Signal Path is unapologetically pro-curiosity. Radio is one of the most rewarding things you can learn, and the Flipper Zero is a wonderful teacher. We want you poking at every signal in your home, saving them, understanding them, and finishing the capstone with a complete atlas of your own RF world.

We're just as unapologetic about the line. This course exists to help you understand radio, not to help anyone take what isn't theirs. Every lab uses devices you own. Every transmission goes to your own hardware in your own space. Where a technique could harm others, we explain the concept and stop — no steps, no shortcuts, no "for educational purposes" wink. That's not us being cautious for its own sake; it's what makes it possible to teach this openly at all.
